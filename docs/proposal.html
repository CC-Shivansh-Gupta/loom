Loom: a digital twin for the assembly line you actually have

Most vehicle assembly lines are partly blind — legacy stations on manual checklists next to fully instrumented cells — and every twin on the market assumes they are not. Loom is a live twin that says what it measured, what it inferred and what it forecast; warns minutes before a bottleneck forms even when the failing station has no sensors; holds the exact vehicles a silent drift put at risk; and keeps a public score of its own accuracy.

Business proposalPrototype: 6,000 lines, 57 tests, 17 commits29 August 2026

What we are proposing

A software product, deployed read-only beside an existing line, that gives four roles — floor supervisor, quality engineer, maintenance, plant manager — one consistent picture of the line and of what is about to go wrong, from whatever sensors the line already has. It ships with its own scorekeeper, so the plant can see the twin's lead time and false-alarm rate before trusting it, and an AI layer that writes the briefings, proposes what to try and tunes the system — through a gate it cannot bypass.

The problem, as it exists on the floor

An idle automotive line costs $2.3 million an hour — more than double the 2019 figure — and the average plant now takes 81 minutes to restart after a stoppage (Siemens, True Cost of Downtime 2024). Unplanned downtime drains $1.4 trillion a year from the world's 500 largest companies. The instinctive answer is a digital twin, and automotive has tried: more than 70 % of manufacturers in the sector are piloting or deploying one. Yet 64 % of digital-twin projects never leave pilot, Gartner finds only one in three deployed beyond it, and a 2025 IoT Analytics survey traces 58 % of delays to OT/IT integration.

The reason is in the brief's own words: real lines are a patchwork of legacy and modern equipment with uneven sensor coverage. Some stations stream PLC telemetry; others are a clipboard. A twin that only works when every station reports is a twin for a plant nobody has. Three consequences follow, and they are the three things a plant team most needs to know before they happen:

  • A local slip has line-wide effects. A station running slower than takt starves everything after it and blocks everything before it — but the queue that reveals it forms several stations away, minutes later.
  • Defects are introduced early and surface late. A weld current sagging out of spec has no cycle-time symptom; the weak weld appears at end-of-line inspection eleven vehicles later, and every vehicle built in between carries it.
  • The person deciding is working blind and under time pressure. Hold a batch or let it ship, call a floater or wait — one supervisor, incomplete information, seconds to decide.

The gap. Nothing on this list is built for a line that is partly blind. The high end assumes you can see everything and can afford to model it; the OEE tier assumes a sensor wherever you want an answer. Loom's position is between them: live, configured rather than modelled, honest about what it cannot see, and self-scoring — for the majority of lines, not the showcase.

How Loom works

Four layers, one event schema, and a hard wall between what the plant does and what the twin believes. The wall is the design: the twin can only ever use what the sensor layer passes, so its honesty about coverage is architectural, not a setting.

Flow: warn before the line blocks

The twin reconstructs every vehicle's timeline from the flow rules of a serial line; measurements pin values, inference fills the gaps, and every value carries a mark — measured, inferred or simulated. For a station with no sensors, the rule that does the work is simple and exact: when the next station measurably starts a vehicle after sitting idle, that instant is when the dark station released it. That yields exact cycle samples precisely when the dark station is the bottleneck — the case that matters. A trend fit with standard-error and persistence guards, then a short forward simulation of buffer fill, produces "B2 blocks in ~7 minutes, confidence 70 %, 100 % of evidence inferred". A naïve trend test produced 49 false alarms per shift; the guards brought it to 0.2 per 8 hours, and the tuning table is in the repository.

Quality: catch the drift at source, hold only what is at risk

Process parameters (weld current, torque, booth humidity) are monitored with EWMA/CUSUM charts baselined on the spec. A drift is a warning until a reading actually goes out of spec; that first reading opens a hold back-filled from the estimated onset. When inspections fail without an upstream signal, contribution analysis scores every upstream condition — and pairs of conditions — by lift and Fisher exact test, and returns ranked hypotheses with evidence, never a verdict. Holds list vehicles as sure, uncertain (built inside the onset margin or at a station that does not report) and already exited.

Beyond serial

Parallel stations sharing a queue, rework loops that re-enter inspection out of order, shift calendars with breaks and crew-specific cycle multipliers. The rework scenario shows something every automotive engineer has lived: a quality problem becoming a flow problem, as the inspection station processes fails twice and becomes the bottleneck — and the forecaster names it.

Integration: a read-only tap

Loom subscribes — OPC UA, Modbus, historian, MES events, operator app — and never writes to a PLC. Deployment is a ladder: shadow (the twin runs, nobody sees alerts, the evaluator scores them) → advisory → reversible automatic (flag a batch for extra inspection; nothing that touches the physical line). We proved the tap against Factory I/O, a third-party PLC-driven simulator we did not write, over a real Modbus socket at 50 Hz: every part tracked, a sensorless station reconstructed, a wearing station forecast — zero writes.

Onboarding is a file

The line is data, not code: an ISA-95-shaped YAML with libraries of station types and sensor profiles, and extends: for site variants. A second plant — 30 stations, 4 zones, 3 model variants, 9 stations on checklists or dark — runs on unchanged code and is forecast 10.6 minutes ahead with no false alarms. An AI onboarding assistant drafts the file from a sentence: "18 stations, takt 72 s, 4 manual, 2 dark, paint buffer 10."

Where AI belongs — and where it does not

This is an AI competition, and the brief warns against treating the model as the source of truth. Our rule, enforced by every module: statistics and simulation produce every number; the LLM turns numbers into decisions, proposes hypotheses for the simulator to test, and drives the improvement loop through a gate it cannot bypass.

This is where the market is going — 2026 is the year "agents enter the decision loop" of plant operations, with Deloitte forecasting adoption in manufacturing rising from 6 % to 24 % — and the same sources insist the guardrails, traceability and permission boundaries be part of the technology. In Loom they are the mechanism, not a slide.

Business case

The value model runs live in the product (the Exec view) from the line's measured lead time, hold sizes and escapes, with every assumption printed so a sceptic can change one and redo the arithmetic. Anchors from the research: an idle automotive line costs $2.3 M per hour; a defect caught downstream costs ~10× the point-of-origin fix, ~100× at final assembly, ~1000× in the field; one plant cut a containment exercise from two days of log review to four minutes with genealogy.

We deliberately use a per-line downtime cost of $8,000 per minute — about a fifth of the plant-wide Siemens figure — and a 50 % share of warnings acted on in time. Even at a tenth of the bottleneck line alone, payback is inside a quarter. The claim we make to a plant is not the number; it is that after a two-week shadow period the plant will have its own number, from its own line, in the trust ledger.

Phased roadmap

  1. Pilot line, shadow mode — weeks 0–4Read-only tap on one line (OPC UA/Modbus + MES events). Twin runs; alerts hidden; evaluator scores lead time and false alarms against what actually happened. Deliverable: the plant's own trust ledger.
  2. Advisory — weeks 4–10Alerts and holds go to the supervisor and quality screens with evidence and confidence. Operators confirm or dismiss; dismissals recalibrate. First value-of-information retrofits in an existing maintenance window.
  3. Reversible automation — weeks 10–16Pre-approved, reversible actions run automatically: flag a batch for extra inspection, open a hold. Anything touching the physical line stays with a person.
  4. Second line, second plant — quarter 2A new line is a plant file. Libraries of station types and sensor profiles are shared; thresholds self-calibrate per station. The onboarding assistant drafts the file; the engineer reviews it.
  5. Product — quarter 3 onwardHistorian-backed store, plant SSO for the audit trail, IATF 16949 retention, Claude on-prem or VPC for the AI layer, fleet view across plants.

OT security and change controlRead-only, on-prem edge, no PLC writes; retrofits only in scheduled windows; every recommendation and override audited

What we do not claim

  • Rework breaks first-in-first-out order; inference at a sensorless station downstream of a rework loop is exact only between rework points.
  • Two adjacent finish-only stations cannot be told apart; the twin abstains rather than guess.
  • The demo line sits at the false-alarm budget, not under it; the 30-station plant sits under it.
  • The Factory I/O run used a stand-in controller; the real scene needs one round of adjustment.
  • Process parameters do not come out of a Factory I/O scene, so the quality side is proven on the simulated plant only.

What we ask

One line, one shadow period, four weeks. We bring the tap, the twin and the scorekeeper; the plant brings the sensors it already has. At the end the plant owns a number nobody had to take on faith.
